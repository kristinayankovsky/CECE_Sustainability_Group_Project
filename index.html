Project Proposal

Our project focuses on responsible fishing and sustainable seafood consumption in Washington State. We want to help people understand where their seafood comes from, how it is harvested, and how to reduce food waste.

We propose creating an educational website and brochure, along with a short presentation for a local fishing organization or community recreation program. These materials would explain responsible harvesting, seafood sourcing, and practical ways to waste less food.

Goal: Encourage informed seafood choices and fishing practices that support the long-term health of Washington's fisheries.

Target audience: Local fishing organizations, community recreation programs, and people interested in responsible seafood consumption.

Sustainable Fishing and Seafood Practices

1. Follow current fishing regulations

Before fishing, check Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife requirements for licenses, species, seasons, catch limits, and allowed gear. Check emergency rule changes for the specific location, and learn the applicable retention and release requirements.

2. Know where seafood comes from

Ask vendors about the species, harvest location, and how the seafood was caught or farmed. Learn about the processors, distributors, and transportation involved. A product being local does not automatically mean it is sustainable.

3. Reduce food waste

Buy or legally harvest only what you can use. Plan meals, keep seafood safely chilled, and freeze suitable extra portions promptly. Follow official food-safety guidance when handling and storing seafood.

4. Understand fish habitats

Fish migration depends on connected waterways. NOAA explains that barriers can interrupt migration and that fish ladders can help fish pass certain obstructions. Learning about habitat is part of understanding sustainable fisheries.

Research and Next Steps

  1. Contact organizations. Email local fishing organizations, tribal fisheries departments, and seafood vendors to ask about their practices and possible presentation dates.
  2. Study seafood sourcing. Ask where seafood is harvested, how it is transported and stored, and what information supports sustainability claims.
  3. Compare salmon costs. Compare the same species, cut, and fresh or frozen condition. Record prices per pound and sourcing information. For recreational catch, consider license, gear, travel, and storage costs.
  4. Prepare and present. Use the research to complete the website and brochure, present the proposal to an interested organization, and gather feedback.

Project status: Outreach, interviews, and price comparisons are planned. No organizational partnership or research findings are confirmed. Treaty tribes are fisheries co-managers; any invited representatives would share their own perspectives.
